# Liquid Neural Networks, Explained for Law

> Archived July 22, 2026. Superseded by `docs/primer.md`.

*A primer on the continuous-time models coming out of Daniela Rus's lab at
MIT CSAIL — what they are, how they differ from LSTMs and Transformers, and
why they might matter for litigation finance. Written to be built upon:
diagram slots are marked and specced.*

---

## The problem nobody's model was built for

A legal case does not arrive on a clock.

Court filings, motions, discovery events, and settlement talks land at uneven
intervals — three events in a week, then silence for eight months, then a
trial date and everything accelerates. Almost every sequence model in modern
AI assumes the opposite: discrete, regular ticks (an LSTM's steps) or a bag
of tokens with positional encodings (a Transformer). Both can be *told* how
much time passed, as a feature. Neither *feels* it.

A lawyer feels it. A case that has sat dormant for eight months is a
different object than the same case dormant for eight days — the meaning of
the gap itself carries information. That gap is where liquid neural networks
live.

## The lineage: from a worm's brain to continuous-time AI

The models trace to Daniela Rus's group at MIT CSAIL, principally Ramin
Hasani and Mathias Lechner (with Alexander Amini and others), and draw their
inspiration from an unlikely place: the 302-neuron nervous system of
*C. elegans*, a microscopic worm that produces surprisingly rich behavior
with almost no neurons. The research question: what if individual artificial
neurons had that kind of rich, continuous internal dynamics, instead of the
static activation functions we've used for decades?

Three papers define the lineage:

- **Neural Circuit Policies (NCP, 2020, *Nature Machine Intelligence*)** —
  sparse, structured wirings between neurons that famously drove a car with a
  19-neuron policy. The point isn't minimalism for its own sake: a 19-neuron
  network is small enough that a human can trace *why* it decided something.
- **Liquid Time-Constant Networks (LTC, 2021, *AAAI*)** — neurons whose
  internal "speed" (time constant) is not fixed but changes as a function of
  their inputs, governed by an ordinary differential equation. The network's
  state doesn't just update when an event arrives; it *evolves during the
  gap* between events, at a rate the network itself controls.
- **Closed-form Continuous-time Networks (CfC, 2022, *Nature Machine
  Intelligence*)** — a closed-form approximation of the LTC dynamics that
  keeps the expressiveness but removes the expensive ODE solver, making the
  models practical to train.

The lineage has since left the lab: Liquid AI, a startup founded by the same
researchers, is commercializing liquid-based foundation models — the first
credible non-Transformer architecture family at LLM scale. The open-source
`ncps` library is the reference implementation of the research models.

## LSTM vs. Transformer vs. Liquid — three ways to read a docket

**An LSTM reads the docket like a checklist.** One event per step, in order.
At each step, learned gates decide what to remember, what to forget, and what
to output. It's sequential and causal — good properties — but the network has
no clock: the eight-month gap and the eight-day gap are both just "the next
row." You can hand it the gap length as a feature, and practitioners do, but
the memory itself doesn't age.

**A Transformer reads the whole file at once.** No recurrence at all —
attention lets every event look directly at every other event, which is why
Transformers train so well in parallel and dominate at scale. But "time" is
just position: an index, or an embedding. The model must *learn* from data
that position 12 minus position 5 means something — it isn't built into the
dynamics. And the price of that power is size, data hunger, and a decision
process that is genuinely hard to audit.

**A liquid model ages between filings.** Between events, each neuron's state
relaxes toward an input-dependent equilibrium at its own learned rate — some
neurons are "fast" (forget quickly), some "slow" (hold long context), and
critically, *the rates themselves respond to what's happening*. A dormant
case literally decays certain neurons while others hold. The elapsed time
isn't a feature appended to the input; it's an argument to the dynamics.

The one-line version for a general audience: **an LSTM counts events, a
Transformer reads the room, a liquid network watches the clock.**

## "Should we have just used a Transformer?" — an honest answer

This question came up while building `liquid-legal`, in the specific form of:
*should the baseline have been an encoder-decoder Transformer, ModernBERT, or
something from the Gemma/T5 family?* It deserves a straight answer, and the
answer starts with a category correction that matters for everything else
here.

Those three names are three different things. **ModernBERT** is an
encoder-only model — it turns text into embeddings for classification and
retrieval; it doesn't generate or model sequences forward in time. **Gemma**
is a full decoder LLM — a general-purpose text generator. **T5** is an
encoder-decoder built to transduce one sequence into another. All three are
*text* models. Our task is not text understanding. A docket trajectory is 16
event types, a handful of covariates, and irregular timestamps — you do not
need a hundred million parameters and a 32,000-token vocabulary to process 16
symbols, and a model that large brings real costs: data hunger (thousands of
labeled case outcomes is a lot to ask), compute, and an audit surface no
regulator will accept for capital-allocation decisions.

There are, however, three places Transformers genuinely belong in this stack,
and pretending otherwise would be spin:

1. **Upstream**: reading raw docket *text* and extracting structured events
   (motion type, outcome, amounts) — a ModernBERT-style encoder is exactly
   the right tool. Liquid models start where that pipeline ends.
2. **As a baseline**: a temporal Transformer over the event stream is a fair
   architectural comparison, and we ran it — at matched parameter budget it
   lands between the LSTM and the liquid models on synthetic data (0.909
   settlement AUC), and handles hidden-latent conditions best of all three.
3. **Downstream**: LLM agents that *consume* the trajectory model's outputs —
   the liquid engine maintains a compact, continuously evolving state of the
   case; the LLM reasons over it, drafts, negotiates. The liquid model
   remembers; the LLM thinks.

The architecture story is not "liquid beats Transformer." It's: right-sized,
auditable dynamics for the temporal core, Transformers where language is
actually involved.

## What we found when we built it

We built `liquid-legal`, an open-source package that trains these models on
litigation event streams — first on a synthetic docket generator with known
ground truth, benchmarked honestly against an LSTM, a compact time-aware
Transformer, and gradient-boosted trees on hand-engineered features. The
short version of the results, because the details deserve their own write-up:

- **On synthetic data, the liquid model does not win** — XGBoost on
  engineered features tops the board (0.917), the LSTM leads the neural
  models (0.914 vs 0.871 settlement AUC), and the gap is robust to training
  budget. We publish those numbers as-is, because they're the numbers that
  are true.
- **The ablation tells you why**: the synthetic world hands every model the
  latent traits that cause its irregular timing, so the timestamps are
  redundant and *no* time-aware architecture earns anything. Hide those
  covariates and timing becomes decisive — but attention exploits it better
  than liquid dynamics. That conditional is the real finding, and real
  dockets are the only court that can rule on it.
- **The auditability argument, by contrast, is demonstrably real**: the
  liquid model is the smallest in the matrix (16k parameters, 86% of
  synapses pruned), and gradient saliency shows it attends to exactly the
  events the legal domain says should matter — dispositive motions,
  discovery stalls — and *more* to stalls for under-equipped plaintiffs,
  matching the mechanism we built into the world. Explanations you can check
  against ground truth are rare in this field.
- **The decisive question is open**: whether native continuous-time handling
  pays off on real dockets, where timestamps carry information that event
  types alone don't. That's what real data will answer, and it's the next
  experiment.

One more finding, about process rather than models: the interpretability
tooling caught a semantic bug in our own data generator that every predictive
benchmark had silently tolerated. The explanation found what accuracy could
not. If you take one sentence from this primer, take that one.

## Where this goes

The near-term agenda is concrete: a real-docket adapter (CourtListener/RECAP)
to settle the open question; the counterfactual probe as a product —
*same case, different judge* — which is the query a litigation funder
actually wants to run; and a portfolio risk monitor that watches a book of
funded cases as continuously evolving trajectories rather than quarterly PDF
reports.

The broader bet: legal time series are exactly the kind of data the current
AI mainstream handles worst — irregular, regime-shifting, small-n, and
high-stakes — and exactly the kind the liquid lineage was built for. Somebody
was going to find out whether that bet pays. We intend to be the ones who
ran the experiment honestly.
